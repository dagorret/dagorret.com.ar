**Total fertility**, measured in births per woman, differs from the annual birth rate per thousand people. Here we explore fertility and generational replacement: what happens to younger cohorts when a rate persists for decades.

## One initial pyramid, different rates

Presets for South Korea, Japan, the European Union, and Latin America and the Caribbean use World Bank fertility observations for 2023. Only fertility changes: all start with the same synthetic population. This isolates its effect without presenting the diagram as an actual national population pyramid.

## The replacement threshold

In low-mortality populations, approximately $2.1$ births per woman is a common replacement reference without migration. It is not universal: survival and sex ratios matter. Persistently lower fertility narrows new cohorts, but does not imply an immediate collapse of total population.

This experiment calibrates replacement exactly at $f_*=2.1$. Ages advance in five-year steps with fixed survival. For cohort size $n_i$ and survival $s_i$:

$$
n_{i+1}(t+5)=s_i n_i(t).
$$

New births depend on ages 20–44, using synthetic reproductive weights $w_i$:

$$
n_0(t+5)=100\,\frac{f}{2.1}\,\frac{\sum_i w_i n_i(t)}{\sum_i w_i n_i(0)}.
$$

The initial distribution is stationary at $f=2.1$. There is no migration, survival is identical across presets, sexes are split 50/50, and the model ends at ages 85–89. The reference line, initial outlines, and indices help distinguish relative ageing from absolute shrinkage. Horizontal scale adjusts to the scenario; use the indices and table to compare magnitudes.

**Preset source:** [World Bank, total fertility rate](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SP.DYN.TFRT.IN), indicator SP.DYN.TFRT.IN, 2023 observations rounded to two decimals. “Europe” means the EU here, and “Latin America” includes the Caribbean. The horizon is simulated time from an invented population, not a dated forecast.
